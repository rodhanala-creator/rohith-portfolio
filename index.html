ProjectOpsuite
RoleProduct Design · Workflow Strategy
TypeInternal Operations Product
FocusDigital Product Design & Development

Designing an operations dashboard that made service delivery easier to see and manage.

Opsuite was created as a product concept for service-led teams handling enquiries, active jobs, internal handoffs, and task dependencies across multiple channels. The core challenge was not a lack of tools, but a lack of calm. Information lived in too many places, ownership was unclear, and teams were spending too much time checking status instead of moving work forward. [web:87][web:85]

Overview

The project explored how a single product interface could unify enquiry tracking, project status, next actions, and internal visibility. The goal was to create a digital product that reduced ambiguity and gave teams one shared operating view. [web:87][web:89]

What this project demonstrates

Product framing, UX structure, dashboard design, system thinking, front-end planning, and interface decisions built around business usability rather than visual decoration. [web:85][page:0]

The challenge

Service businesses often grow faster than their internal systems. New enquiries sit in one place, jobs are managed in another, follow-ups happen in messages, and no single view shows what needs attention now. That creates small delays, inconsistent handoffs, and too much dependency on memory. [web:87][web:89]

  • Team members lacked one shared view of current workload.
  • Urgent items blended with routine ones.
  • Enquiry-to-delivery stages were visible only through manual checking.
  • Managers had limited clarity on bottlenecks and pending actions.

Role and responsibilities

The work covered product definition, workflow mapping, low-fidelity planning, UI direction, information architecture, and front-end implementation thinking. The aim was to shape a product that felt operationally useful before it felt visually polished. [web:87]

Owned

Problem framing, UX structure, screen hierarchy, dashboard modules, and interface direction.

Designed for

Small teams handling incoming enquiries, active jobs, project status, and internal task coordination.

Process

The process started by reducing the system into four core moments: capture, prioritise, act, and review. That simplified the product direction and made it easier to decide which modules mattered on the main dashboard. [web:81][web:87]

Dashboard wireframe direction Low fidelity
Decision flow System logic
CaptureNew request enters one central intake.
PrioritiseStatus, urgency, and owner become visible.
AssignTask is routed to the next responsible person.
TrackProgress updates stay visible across stages.
ReviewExceptions and delays are surfaced early.
CloseCompleted work feeds reporting and insights.

Solution

The final direction centred on a dashboard-first interface with status cards, stage visibility, ownership markers, and clear task progression. Rather than overwhelming users with filters and tables, the interface focused on what needed action now and what was drifting. [web:85][web:87]

Product principles

Reduce ambiguity, surface priority, preserve context, and make ownership impossible to miss.

Interface choices

Clear hierarchy, modular cards, stage grouping, and an emphasis on scannability over density.

Outcome

The project demonstrates the ability to turn operational friction into a structured product direction, bridging business process, UX architecture, and implementation logic. It works as a portfolio case study for digital product design, internal tools, and design-to-development thinking. [web:87][web:89]

ProjectCoordFlow
RoleDigital Project Manager · Process Designer
TypeDelivery Framework
FocusDigital Project Management

Structuring digital delivery so stakeholders, tasks, and timelines moved with less friction.

CoordFlow is a project-management-led case study focused on the reality that digital work often breaks down not because teams lack skill, but because coordination is fragmented. Updates become informal, ownership gets diluted, and momentum slows each time context is lost. [web:87][web:83]

Project context

The framework was designed for digital work spanning design, build, review, revision, and implementation support. The goal was to create a system that helped projects move clearly between stages without depending on repeated clarification. [file:1][file:2]

What this project demonstrates

Planning discipline, delivery thinking, process clarity, communication design, and the ability to build systems around execution rather than just design outputs. [file:1][file:2]

The problem

Digital projects become heavier when stakeholders are uncertain about current status, pending dependencies, or who owns the next step. This leads to delays that are operational rather than technical. [web:83][web:87]

  • Status lived across chats, emails, and separate documents.
  • Scope changes were not always translated into visible delivery actions.
  • Review cycles lacked one dependable rhythm.
  • Accountability weakened when ownership was not explicit.

Approach

The process started by turning delivery into a visible structure: intake, define, design, review, build, validate, handover. Each stage had its own objective, owner, and exit condition. [web:81][web:87]

Delivery board concept Framework
DefineScope, goals, stakeholders.
DesignOutputs, reviews, feedback.
BuildImplementation and QA path.
ValidateCheck usability and completion.
HandoverAssets, notes, next steps.
SupportPost-launch issue visibility.
Planning artifacts Documentation

Execution model

The most important change was not a prettier board. It was a more reliable project rhythm: clear owners, visible review points, structured updates, and easier escalation when something blocked delivery. [web:83][web:87]

Stakeholder layer

Updates were framed around decisions needed, progress made, and next actions rather than generic status reporting.

Delivery layer

Tasks were grouped by stage and dependency, making work easier to prioritise and hand over between contributors.

Outcome

This case study shows digital project management as a design problem in its own right: how work moves, how information is structured, and how teams keep momentum without confusion. That makes it relevant for project manager roles, delivery roles, and operationally aware digital positions. [file:1][file:2][web:83]

ProjectAI Front Desk
RoleAutomation Strategist · System Designer
TypeAI Intake System
FocusAI Systems & Automation

Designing an AI-assisted intake system that reduced repetitive admin and improved response quality.

AI Front Desk was developed as a practical automation concept for businesses handling repetitive enquiries, scheduling logic, information capture, and internal routing. The aim was not to replace people, but to remove predictable admin so staff could spend more time on higher-value decisions. [file:2][web:79]

Why this project mattered

Many businesses know they want automation, but what they actually need is a cleaner front-end system: one that captures consistent information, routes it properly, and creates dependable next steps. This case study focuses on that layer. [web:79][web:87]

What this project demonstrates

Automation strategy, AI-assisted workflow design, user input design, decision logic, and system architecture oriented toward real operational relief. [file:2][web:79]

The challenge

Without structure, enquiries arrive with missing context, responses vary in quality, and teams repeat the same qualification steps manually. That wastes time and introduces inconsistency. [web:79][web:87]

  • Staff repeated the same intake questions across channels.
  • Required context was often incomplete at the point of handoff.
  • Routing decisions relied too much on individual judgement under time pressure.
  • Follow-up quality varied depending on who handled the enquiry first.

System design

The solution focused on a structured intake flow supported by AI where helpful: capturing information, classifying request type, suggesting next actions, and passing cleaner inputs into the rest of the workflow. [web:79][web:87]

Conversation and routing flow Automation map
CollectCapture the right details through structured prompts.
ClassifyIdentify type, urgency, and route.
SuggestGenerate the next best internal action.
NotifyAlert the right person with better context.
TrackStore status and visible follow-up stage.
ImproveRefine logic based on real usage patterns.
Interface concept Operator view

UX and operational decisions

The design avoided making AI feel magical or opaque. Instead, it treated AI as a structured assistant inside a controlled workflow. Inputs, classifications, and next steps were designed to remain visible and understandable to the team using them. [web:79][web:87]

Experience principle

Make the automation feel reliable, reviewable, and easy to override when needed.

Operational principle

Reduce repeated admin, increase consistency, and improve handoff quality without removing human control.

Outcome

This project shows AI systems work as a combination of product design, process clarity, and implementation logic. It is especially strong for positioning around AI automation, internal tools, and workflow systems rather than generic AI branding. [file:2][web:79]

ProjectLaunchKit
RoleDesigner · Developer · Systems Thinker
TypeReusable Delivery Framework
FocusCross-service Productised Delivery

Creating a reusable launch framework that sped up delivery without making the work feel generic.

LaunchKit explored how digital product thinking could be used to create more repeatable delivery for service businesses. Rather than building every project from zero, the idea was to develop a structured foundation for landing pages, enquiry systems, onboarding logic, and front-end implementation patterns that could be adapted with speed and clarity. [web:88][web:87]

Project objective

The aim was to make digital delivery more scalable while protecting quality. That required a framework strong enough to accelerate the early stages of design and build, but flexible enough to avoid a templated feel. [web:88][web:85]

What this project demonstrates

Productisation strategy, reusable UX patterns, front-end consistency, and the ability to build delivery systems that support multiple service lines. [web:88][web:85]

The problem

Starting from scratch every time slows delivery and makes consistency harder. But overly rigid systems create generic outputs that do not reflect the business properly. The challenge was to find the middle ground. [web:88][web:85]

  • Repeated work across similar project types.
  • Inconsistent structure between design and build phases.
  • Longer delivery timelines caused by avoidable rework.
  • Need for reuse without losing brand or UX specificity.

Framework design

The framework focused on reusable sections, enquiry components, onboarding logic, design tokens, and implementation-ready structures that could speed up delivery while still being reshaped for different contexts. [web:88][web:87]

Design and development thinking

LaunchKit treated reuse as a product design challenge, not a shortcut. The system needed repeatable layout logic, consistent hierarchy, cleaner code organisation, and better alignment between content structure and interface design. [web:85][web:88]

Design layer

Shared layout principles, content structure, conversion paths, and adaptable UI modules.

Development layer

Reusable code patterns, section systems, and implementation decisions designed to speed iteration.

Outcome

This case study is useful because it connects design, development, and service scalability in one project. It shows how product thinking can create a more repeatable and commercially useful delivery model without weakening quality. [web:88][web:85]
